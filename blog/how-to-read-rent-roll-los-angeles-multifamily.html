How to Read a Rent Roll Before Buying a Los Angeles Multifamily Property

By Paul Adams II · Los Angeles Real Estate Advisor · September 4, 2026

A rent roll looks simple.

Unit number. Tenant. Monthly rent. Maybe a move-in date and security deposit.

That simplicity is exactly why buyers sometimes give it too much trust.

For a Los Angeles multifamily acquisition, the rent roll should be treated as the beginning of income due diligence, not proof of income.

A useful rent-roll analysis asks three questions: What is the property collecting today? Is that income documented? What assumptions about future income are actually realistic?

Those questions can materially change what a property is worth.

What is a rent roll?

A rent roll is a unit-by-unit summary of rental information for a property.

Depending on the owner or property manager, it may include unit number, tenant name, bedroom count, monthly rent, lease start date, lease expiration, security deposit, concessions, parking income, storage income, and other charges.

For a small property, the rent roll may be a spreadsheet with four rows. For a larger apartment building, it can contain dozens or hundreds of units.

The basic purpose is the same: summarize the property's rental relationships.

The problem is that a spreadsheet can summarize almost anything. You still need to verify it.

Start with current contract rent

The first number I want is the actual monthly rent owed under each tenancy.

Do not begin with advertised market rents. Do not begin with what a renovated unit nearby rented for last month. Do not begin with the seller's projected rent.

Start with the contract.

If a four-unit property shows rents of $1,500, $1,900, $2,300, and $2,500, the current scheduled monthly rent is $8,200.

That is the base case. Potential market rent is a separate analysis.

This distinction is particularly important in Los Angeles because regulatory status and existing tenancies can affect how quickly current rents can change.

Compare the rent roll to the leases

For every occupied unit, the lease or rental agreement should support the rent roll.

Check the tenant name, unit, rent, lease term, deposit, included utilities, parking, pets, concessions, and unusual clauses.

If the rent roll says $2,300 but the lease says $2,150, you need an explanation. Perhaps there was a lawful increase. Perhaps there is an addendum. Perhaps the rent roll is simply wrong.

The goal is not to assume misconduct. The goal is to reconcile the records before you value the income.

Then compare the rent roll to the tenant ledger

The lease tells you what the tenant owes. The ledger tells you what has actually happened.

Those are different questions.

A tenant may have a $2,000 contractual rent but consistently pay late. Another may owe a balance. Another may have a payment plan or credit.

A seller's rent roll can therefore show $24,000 of annual contractual income for a unit that did not produce $24,000 of cash.

That difference matters. When analyzing income property, I want to understand both scheduled rent and collections.

Verify security deposits

Deposits are not operating income. They are liabilities held in connection with the tenancy and should be properly documented and transferred through the transaction.

Compare the deposit listed on the rent roll with the lease and seller records.

For a ten-unit building, a few discrepancies can add up quickly. The more important issue is what the inconsistency tells you about record quality.

If deposits, rents, or tenancy dates do not reconcile cleanly, that may justify deeper review elsewhere.

Look at move-in dates

Move-in dates are strategically important.

They help you understand the age of the tenancy and can provide context for why rents differ between units.

Two identical apartments may have dramatically different rents because one tenant moved in recently and another has occupied the unit for many years.

That difference should not automatically be treated as "lost income." It is simply part of the property's existing operating reality.

The buyer then needs to understand which regulations apply and what rent adjustments are legally available over time.

For City of Los Angeles properties, the Rent Stabilization Ordinance can govern allowable increases and other tenancy rules for covered units.

Identify every form of income

Rent is not always the only income.

A multifamily property might also receive income from parking, garages, storage, laundry, utility reimbursements, pet charges, furnished-unit premiums, or other services.

But do not automatically capitalize every dollar listed by the seller.

Ask whether the income is recurring, documented, transferable, and legally collectible.

For example, laundry income generated by seller-owned machines is different from revenue under a third-party laundry contract. Parking income tied to a written agreement may be different from an informal monthly arrangement.

The detail matters.

Separate legal rent from market rent

This is one of the most important distinctions in Los Angeles multifamily underwriting.

A seller may present an "upside" rent roll showing both current rents and projected market rents.

That can be useful. It can also be misleading if the buyer treats the projected rents as near-term income.

Suppose a unit currently rents for $1,600 and the seller believes the market rent is $2,600. The spreadsheet may show $1,000 of monthly upside.

But when can you actually collect $2,600? Under what circumstances? What regulations apply? Does the tenant intend to leave? Would renovations be required? How much would those renovations cost? How long would the unit be vacant?

Those questions determine whether the $1,000 difference has immediate economic value, long-term economic value, or very little value at all.

Check whether the property is properly registered

For City of Los Angeles rental housing, registration compliance should be part of due diligence.

Los Angeles Housing Department materials state that landlords of units subject to the Rent Stabilization Ordinance must provide required rent and tenancy information as part of annual registration. Registration status should therefore be examined rather than assumed.

A buyer should review the available property records and ask for the seller's registration documentation when applicable.

Look for concessions and side agreements

Not every economic term appears in the headline rent.

A tenant might have one free month, discounted parking, a recurring credit, landlord-paid utilities, a verbal agreement, a reduced rent tied to services, or an unresolved repair credit.

These arrangements can change effective income. They can also create disputes after closing if the buyer was unaware of them.

Ask for written documentation and relevant tenant correspondence.

Understand who pays utilities

Two buildings with the same gross rents can have very different economics.

If one landlord pays water, gas, electricity, trash, and common-area utilities while another passes some expenses through to tenants where lawful, their NOI will differ materially.

The rent roll alone will not tell you the full story. That is why it should be analyzed alongside the trailing operating statement.

Compare the rent roll to bank deposits

For investment property, documentation becomes stronger when different records tell the same story.

If the rent roll says the property collects $18,000 per month, the leases support those rents, the tenant ledgers show payments, and deposits broadly reconcile with collections, confidence increases.

If the records diverge, the buyer needs to understand why.

This is especially important when purchasing from a small owner who may have managed the building informally. Informal management does not necessarily mean the property is bad. It means diligence may require more work.

Build three rent scenarios

Scenario 1: Current

Use actual existing rents. This tells you what you are buying today.

Scenario 2: Stabilized

Include realistic lawful rent growth, known vacancies, planned renovations, and reasonable leasing assumptions. This tells you what the property could produce after executing a credible business plan.

Scenario 3: Market potential

Use full market rents. This tells you the theoretical ceiling under today's market conditions.

The mistake is using Scenario 3 as if it were Scenario 1. That can make an expensive property look deceptively attractive.

How does the rent roll affect value?

Income-producing real estate is partly valued based on income.

If the rent roll is overstated, unsupported, or dependent on unrealistic future assumptions, your estimated NOI will also be overstated. That flows directly into cap rate and valuation.

For example, capitalizing $20,000 of income that does not actually exist can materially distort what you believe the property is worth.

The exact impact depends on the property and market. The principle does not.

Bad income assumptions produce bad valuations.

A practical rent-roll checklist

Before removing an income-related contingency, I would want to reconcile:

  • current rent for every unit
  • lease documents
  • tenant ledgers
  • payment status
  • security deposits
  • lease expiration dates
  • tenancy start dates
  • concessions
  • parking and storage income
  • utility arrangements
  • property registration
  • pending rent increases
  • notices and disputes
  • vacancies
  • market-rent assumptions

Then I would build the investment model. Not the other way around.

The rent roll is a risk document, not just an income document

A sophisticated buyer reads a rent roll for more than revenue.

It can reveal concentration risk, long-term tenancies, unusually low rents, near-term lease expirations, inconsistent documentation, management problems, and opportunities.

That is what makes it valuable.

A good multifamily acquisition is not built on the highest theoretical rent. It is built on understanding what the property actually is, what it actually earns, and what can realistically change.

Frequently asked questions

Is a rent roll enough to verify rental income?

No. It should be compared with leases, tenant ledgers, deposits, registration records, and operating statements.

Should I value an apartment building using market rent or current rent?

Both can be useful, but for different purposes. Current rent should anchor the base case. Market rent belongs in a separate upside scenario.

Why do move-in dates matter?

They help explain differences between rents and can provide important context for tenancy and regulatory analysis.

Does Los Angeles require landlords to register rental information?

For units subject to the RSO, Los Angeles requires annual rent and tenancy information as part of the Rent Registry process.

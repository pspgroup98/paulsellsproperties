Sawtelle Is Adding Hundreds of Apartments: What Buyers and Owners Should Watch

By Paul Adams II · Los Angeles Real Estate Advisor · September 28, 2026

Sawtelle is quietly undergoing a significant housing buildout.

The latest visible milestone is at 11250 La Grange Avenue, where framing is complete on a five-story, 38-unit apartment building. But that project is only one piece of a much larger development pattern occurring around Sawtelle, the 405 and the Metro E Line.

For buyers and existing property owners, the more useful question is not what one apartment building means. It is what happens when multiple projects begin changing the housing supply and physical character of the same neighborhood at once.

What is being built at 11250 La Grange Avenue?

RBM of California is developing a five-story building containing 38 two- and three-bedroom apartments. The project replaced several single-family homes and will include a two-level underground garage with 100 parking spaces. Five apartments will be reserved for very low-income households as part of the project's density-bonus approvals.

Plans also include a rooftop deck, gym and other common areas. The project is now fully framed, making it much easier to understand its physical scale than when it existed only in plans. But I would not evaluate Sawtelle's development trajectory from this building alone.

How much development is happening in Sawtelle?

Quite a bit. Current projects include new housing at La Grange Avenue, Santa Monica Boulevard, Beloit Avenue, Stoner Avenue, Barrington Avenue, Federal Avenue and around Bundy Drive.

One of the largest development clusters is near Expo/Bundy Station, where three projects are replacing approximately 20 single-family homes with more than 600 apartments. Other projects range from smaller infill buildings with a few dozen units to larger developments with more than 100 apartments.

Taken together, this is more important than any individual construction site. Sawtelle is adding housing at several different scales simultaneously.

Why is Sawtelle attracting apartment development?

Location explains much of it. Sawtelle sits between major Westside employment and residential centers, including Santa Monica, Westwood, Brentwood and Century City.

The neighborhood also has direct freeway access through the 405 and rail access through the Metro E Line. Those characteristics make the area attractive for multifamily development because residents can reach a large number of Westside destinations without living directly inside the most expensive adjacent neighborhoods.

Sawtelle also contains commercial corridors where larger residential buildings fit more naturally than they would on smaller interior residential streets.

Why does the E Line matter?

Transit proximity can influence what can be built. Los Angeles has used programs such as Transit Oriented Communities incentives to allow qualifying projects near major transit to receive additional density, height or other development incentives in exchange for affordable housing requirements.

That is one reason neighborhoods around rail stations can change faster than nearby areas without comparable transit access. For Sawtelle, Expo/Bundy and Expo/Sepulveda create development nodes that did not exist in the same way before the E Line.

That does not mean every nearby parcel will become an apartment building. It does mean buyers should understand the development potential around a property before assuming today's surroundings will remain unchanged.

What should a Sawtelle buyer investigate?

I would pay particular attention to adjacent and nearby parcels. If you are buying a condo with an open western view, what is currently creating that view?

Is it another condominium building that is unlikely to change? A parking lot? A gas station? A one-story commercial building? A group of older houses sitting on commercially zoned land?

Those situations carry very different development risk. A view is not necessarily permanent just because it exists when you tour the property.

Does more apartment construction hurt existing property values?

There is no automatic answer. More housing can create more competition in the rental market, particularly if several similar buildings open at once. But new residents can also support restaurants, retail and neighborhood services.

Development may improve underused commercial corridors while also adding traffic, construction and changes in neighborhood scale. For homeowners, the effect depends heavily on the individual property.

A house on a quiet interior street may interact with development differently than a condo overlooking a redevelopment site. I would avoid broad claims that development is either inherently good or inherently bad for property values. The specific relationship between the property and the development matters.

What about traffic and parking?

These are reasonable concerns in Sawtelle. The neighborhood already experiences congestion because of the 405, major east-west corridors and the concentration of businesses around Sawtelle Boulevard.

New residents add trips. But the amount of traffic generated by a project depends on unit count, parking, resident behavior and access to transit. The La Grange project, for example, includes 100 parking spaces for 38 apartments.

Other transit-oriented projects may include substantially less parking. Buyers should evaluate the actual project rather than assuming every new building has the same parking strategy.

Could additional housing change Sawtelle's commercial corridors?

Potentially. Residential density creates customers within walking distance. That can help support restaurants, cafes, grocery stores and neighborhood services.

Sawtelle already has one of the Westside's strongest dining districts. Additional residential population may increase demand for businesses beyond the established Sawtelle Boulevard restaurant corridor.

That is one reason housing development and commercial development often reinforce each other. The change tends to happen gradually. A new apartment building does not transform a neighborhood by itself. A collection of them can.

What does this mean for investors?

Sawtelle's development pipeline is a reminder to analyze both the existing property and future supply. If you are evaluating an apartment building, you need to know what newer buildings are coming nearby.

New construction can compete with older units for tenants, particularly at the higher end of the rental market. At the same time, older buildings may compete at lower price points that new construction cannot easily match because new projects have much higher development costs.

That distinction matters. More apartments does not necessarily mean every rental property faces the same competitive pressure.

What should sellers know?

New development can become part of the conversation when marketing an existing property. For some buyers, proximity to the E Line, restaurants and increasing neighborhood investment will be appealing. Others may be more sensitive to density or construction.

The strongest marketing does not pretend those tradeoffs do not exist. It explains where the property sits within the neighborhood and identifies what differentiates it.

Is Sawtelle becoming a different neighborhood?

It is changing, but established neighborhoods rarely transform all at once. Sawtelle will continue to have single-family streets, small apartment buildings, larger multifamily projects and commercial corridors.

What is changing is the amount of housing being added along certain streets and near transit. That distinction is important. A neighborhood can become substantially denser without every part of it feeling equally dense.

Frequently asked questions

What is being built at 11250 La Grange Avenue? A five-story development with 38 two- and three-bedroom apartments, five of which are designated for very low-income households.

Is there other apartment construction in Sawtelle? Yes. Multiple projects are underway or planned around Santa Monica Boulevard, Beloit Avenue, Barrington Avenue, Stoner Avenue and the Expo/Bundy area.

Why is so much housing being built near the E Line? Transit access and Los Angeles development incentives can make sites near major transit more attractive for multifamily housing.

Should buyers investigate future development near a condo? Yes. Nearby zoning, underused parcels and active development applications can affect views, privacy, traffic and the future character of the immediate area.

Does new construction automatically reduce nearby home values? No. The effect depends on the type of development, the individual property, location and broader neighborhood conditions.

Paul's take

I think Sawtelle is a neighborhood where buyers need to look beyond the unit they are purchasing. A condo can be excellent and still have development risk next door. A house can be on a quiet street while substantial density is being added only a few blocks away.

Those distinctions are exactly why neighborhood-level due diligence matters. When I evaluate a property in an area with active development, I want to understand not only what exists today, but what could reasonably exist around it several years from now.

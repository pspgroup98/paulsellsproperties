How to Analyze a Duplex in Los Angeles Before You Buy It

By Paul Adams II · Los Angeles Real Estate Advisor · August 28, 2026

A duplex can be one of the most interesting property types in Los Angeles because it sits between a home and a conventional multifamily investment. You can live in one unit and rent the other, rent both units, renovate over time, hold for long-term appreciation, or potentially pursue additional development where the property and regulations allow it.

But none of those possibilities make a duplex a good investment automatically.

The right question is not, "Is buying a duplex in Los Angeles a good idea?"

The better question is, "Does this particular duplex make sense at this price, with these rents, these tenants, these expenses, this financing, and this business plan?"

That is how I would analyze the deal.

Start with the strategy before you start with the spreadsheet

Two buyers can purchase the same duplex and rationally arrive at different valuations because they are pursuing different outcomes.

An owner-occupant may care about reducing the net cost of housing. A long-term investor may prioritize stable cash flow. A value-add investor may be focused on renovation potential. Another buyer may care most about land value or the possibility of creating additional housing.

Before calculating returns, define what success means.

  • Will you occupy one unit?
  • Will both units remain rentals?
  • How long do you expect to hold the property?
  • Do you need immediate cash flow?
  • Are you planning renovations?
  • Are the existing tenants part of the long-term plan?
  • Are you relying on future rent increases to make the numbers work?
  • Are you considering an ADU or other future improvements?

Your answer determines which numbers deserve the most attention.

Step 1: Determine the real gross income

Start with what the property actually produces.

If Unit A rents for $2,400 and Unit B rents for $2,800, the property's current scheduled monthly rent is $5,200, or $62,400 annually.

Do not replace those rents with optimistic market-rent estimates just because comparable listings suggest the units could theoretically rent for more.

Current income and potential income should be modeled separately.

That distinction becomes particularly important with occupied Los Angeles properties where tenant protections may affect the timing and feasibility of rent changes or vacancy.

Step 2: Account for vacancy and collection loss

A spreadsheet that assumes 100 percent rent collection forever is not underwriting. It is a best-case scenario.

Even a well-located property can experience turnover, missed rent, leasing downtime, or repairs between tenants.

If a property produces $62,400 in scheduled annual rent, applying a hypothetical 5 percent vacancy and collection factor would reduce effective rental income by $3,120 before expenses.

The appropriate assumption depends on the property and strategy. The point is to include a realistic allowance rather than pretend occupancy is guaranteed.

Step 3: Identify every operating expense

This is where many first-time investors underestimate ownership costs.

Potential expenses can include:

  • Property taxes
  • Insurance
  • Water and sewer
  • Trash
  • Landscaping
  • Pest control
  • Repairs and maintenance
  • Property management
  • Licensing or registration costs where applicable
  • Common-area electricity or gas
  • Accounting and administrative expenses
  • Reserves for future capital improvements

Some expenses may be paid directly by tenants, while others remain with the owner. Verify the actual arrangement rather than assuming.

Step 4: Calculate net operating income

Net operating income, or NOI, is the income produced by the property after operating expenses but before mortgage payments and income taxes.

The basic formula is:

Effective Gross Income minus Operating Expenses = NOI

Suppose a duplex produces $60,000 in effective annual income after vacancy assumptions and has $20,000 in annual operating expenses.

Its NOI would be $40,000.

That number allows you to compare the property's operating performance independently from how you choose to finance it.

Step 5: Calculate the cap rate, but understand what it tells you

The capitalization rate is:

NOI ÷ Purchase Price = Cap Rate

If the NOI is $40,000 and the purchase price is $1,000,000:

$40,000 ÷ $1,000,000 = 4.0%

That does not tell you whether the property is good or bad.

A cap rate needs context. Location, building quality, tenant profile, growth potential, redevelopment potential, financing environment, deferred maintenance, and investor objectives all influence what return is acceptable.

A lower cap rate property in a highly desirable Los Angeles neighborhood can make sense for one investor and be completely unattractive to another.

Step 6: Add financing

Cap rate ignores your mortgage.

Your actual cash flow does not.

Once debt is introduced, calculate annual debt service and subtract it from NOI.

NOI minus Annual Debt Service = Pre-Tax Cash Flow

A property can have a positive NOI and still produce negative cash flow after financing.

This is particularly important in a high-cost market like Los Angeles, where purchase prices can make highly leveraged acquisitions difficult to cash flow at current rents.

That does not automatically make the investment wrong, especially for an owner-occupant or long-term investor. It does mean you should understand exactly what you are subsidizing and why.

Step 7: Calculate cash-on-cash return

Cash-on-cash return measures annual pre-tax cash flow relative to the cash you actually invested.

The simplified formula is:

Annual Pre-Tax Cash Flow ÷ Total Cash Invested = Cash-on-Cash Return

Total cash invested should not necessarily mean only the down payment.

Include relevant closing costs, immediate repairs, renovation capital, and other cash required to get the property into your intended operating condition.

This makes the metric much more useful when comparing opportunities.

Step 8: Understand the tenants before assigning upside

If the duplex is occupied, tenant due diligence is part of investment due diligence.

Review leases, ledgers, deposits, rent amounts, notices, payment history, and applicable regulatory status.

For City of Los Angeles properties, determine whether the units are covered by the Rent Stabilization Ordinance or other tenant protections. LAHD states that qualifying duplexes first built on or before October 1, 1978 can fall under the RSO.

If your investment thesis requires immediate market rent, vacancy, owner occupancy, or substantial changes to existing tenancies, investigate those assumptions before you buy.

A projected rent increase is not income until it is legally and practically achievable.

Step 9: Inspect the physical asset like an investor

A duplex inspection is not simply a list of things that are broken.

It should feed directly into your financial model.

If the roof has limited remaining life, estimate replacement cost and timing. If electrical, plumbing, HVAC, foundation, drainage, sewer, or building-envelope issues appear, determine how they affect both immediate cash needs and long-term reserves.

I would separate repairs into three categories:

  • Immediate: necessary for safety, habitability, financing, insurance, or preventing further damage.
  • Near-term capital: likely necessary within the next several years.
  • Value-add: discretionary improvements intended to increase rent, utility, or resale value.

This prevents cosmetic renovation ideas from distracting from expensive building systems.

Step 10: Analyze future upside separately

This is where the deal can become interesting, but also where investors can talk themselves into overpaying.

Potential upside might include:

  • Renovating units as legally available
  • Improving operational efficiency
  • Changing management
  • Adding laundry or other amenities
  • Reducing unnecessary expenses
  • Adding an ADU where feasible
  • Improving underutilized outdoor space
  • Long-term rent growth
  • Future resale to another investor or owner-occupant

Run at least two scenarios.

Scenario 1: What happens if none of the upside occurs quickly?

Scenario 2: What happens if the business plan succeeds?

If the acquisition only works financially under Scenario 2, you should understand exactly what must go right.

Do not ignore the exit strategy

The purchase price gets most of the attention, but eventually you may sell.

Think about who the likely future buyer is.

A duplex can potentially appeal to investors, owner-occupants, multigenerational households, and buyers seeking flexible housing configurations. But tenant status, rents, property condition, financing conditions, and regulatory issues can change the future buyer pool.

Your exit strategy should therefore be part of the acquisition decision.

I have written separately about the tradeoffs between selling a Los Angeles duplex vacant and selling it occupied. That decision can materially change how different buyers view the property.

The metric I care about most is not one metric

Investors frequently ask for one number that tells them whether a property is a good deal.

I do not think that number exists.

Cap rate matters. Cash flow matters. Cash-on-cash return matters. Appreciation potential matters. Financing matters. Tenant risk matters. Capital expenditures matter. Location matters. Optionality matters.

The investment decision comes from how those pieces interact.

A duplex producing modest current cash flow might still make sense for an owner-occupant who substantially reduces personal housing costs. The same property might make no sense for an investor whose sole objective is immediate yield.

A simple duplex underwriting checklist

  • Purchase price
  • Actual current rents
  • Market rents
  • Vacancy assumption
  • Operating expenses
  • NOI
  • Cap rate
  • Loan amount and interest rate
  • Annual debt service
  • Cash flow
  • Total cash invested
  • Cash-on-cash return
  • Tenant and regulatory status
  • Immediate repairs
  • Capital reserves
  • Potential value-add strategy
  • Downside scenario
  • Exit strategy

Once those pieces are on the table, you can evaluate the property as an investment rather than simply as a listing.

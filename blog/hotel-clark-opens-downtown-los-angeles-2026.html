Hotel Clark Finally Opens in Downtown Los Angeles: Inside a Decades-Long Adaptive Reuse Story

By Paul Adams II · Los Angeles Real Estate Advisor · October 7, 2026

One of Downtown Los Angeles' strangest unfinished real-estate stories finally has an ending.

Hotel Clark has soft-opened at 426 S. Hill Street, bringing a long-dormant historic building back into active use with 348 guest rooms. The hotel is now accepting guests after an unusually long redevelopment history in which renovation work was largely completed years before the building actually opened.

For Downtown, the significance is not simply another hotel.

It is the reactivation of a large historic property that spent years sitting essentially finished but unused.

What is Hotel Clark?

Hotel Clark is an 11-story historic hotel on Hill Street in Downtown's Historic Core.

The current property contains 348 modernized rooms and sits near Pershing Square and several of Downtown's major civic, cultural and commercial destinations.

The building originally contained more than 500 guestrooms and dates to the early twentieth century.

Its history since then has been unusually complicated.

Why did Hotel Clark take so long to reopen?

The property went through decades of changing ownership, abandoned plans and redevelopment attempts.

At one point it was acquired by the People's Republic of China with plans for a business and cultural center. That proposal did not materialize.

Later redevelopment efforts converted the property back toward hotel use.

The unusual part is that much of the interior renovation was reportedly finished by 2014, yet the building remained closed for roughly another decade.

That makes the opening particularly interesting from a real-estate perspective. Construction completion does not necessarily mean a property has achieved economic activation.

What does the reopened hotel include?

In addition to 348 guestrooms, current property information describes more than 11,000 square feet of meeting and event space, a second-floor pool deck, on-site parking and multiple food-and-beverage concepts.

Its restaurant, Votana, is expected to have a larger opening celebration October 15.

The hotel is still in a soft-opening phase, which means operations and individual amenities may continue evolving.

That is worth remembering if you plan to visit immediately.

Why does adaptive reuse matter Downtown?

Downtown Los Angeles contains a large inventory of historic commercial buildings that would be extremely difficult to reproduce today.

Adaptive reuse gives some of those buildings a new economic purpose without erasing the architecture that makes them distinctive.

Hotels, apartments and other uses can sometimes work in buildings that no longer function competitively for their original purpose.

The process is not always easy. Older buildings can present structural, mechanical, accessibility and floor-plan challenges.

Hotel Clark demonstrates another risk: a redevelopment can consume enormous time and capital before the building produces income.

Why is the location interesting?

Hotel Clark sits on Hill Street near Pershing Square.

That places it within walking distance of portions of the Historic Core, Grand Central Market, Broadway, Bunker Hill and Downtown's civic center.

It also places the hotel near rail transit.

For a visitor, that creates a very different experience from staying at a freeway-oriented hotel.

The surrounding neighborhood becomes part of the stay.

What can a hotel add to a neighborhood?

Hotels create a rotating population of visitors.

Those visitors eat at restaurants, buy coffee, attend events, shop and use transportation.

A functioning 348-room property therefore has the potential to generate more street-level economic activity than an empty building.

That does not mean one hotel can solve Downtown's larger challenges. It cannot.

But active buildings are generally more useful to a neighborhood than dormant ones.

Does this mean Downtown Los Angeles is back?

I would avoid that framing.

Downtown is too large and complicated to describe through one opening.

Different districts have different conditions.

Office vacancy, homelessness, public safety, residential demand, tourism, transit and entertainment activity all interact differently across Downtown.

Hotel Clark reopening is a positive piece of information. It is not proof that every Downtown real-estate problem has been resolved.

That distinction matters when evaluating property.

What does this tell us about vacant buildings?

An empty building is not necessarily an easy redevelopment opportunity.

A property may look underused from the street while carrying substantial structural, legal, financing or operational complications.

That is particularly true with historic buildings.

For investors, the lesson is straightforward.

The purchase price is only the beginning. You need to understand what it will take to place the property into productive use.

Could the hotel help nearby businesses?

Potentially.

Hundreds of hotel rooms create a built-in customer base for food, entertainment and services.

Guests may visit Grand Central Market, nearby restaurants, museums, events and other Downtown destinations.

Meeting and event space can also bring visitors who are not staying overnight.

The economic effect will depend on occupancy and how guests actually use the surrounding neighborhood.

But converting a closed building into an occupied hotel creates opportunities that did not exist when the property was dark.

What about residential buyers Downtown?

I would not use the hotel opening as a reason by itself to purchase a condo.

Instead, I would treat it as another data point when evaluating the trajectory of the immediate area.

What businesses are opening? What buildings remain vacant? How does the block feel during the day and at night? How close is transit? What construction is underway? What are HOA finances and reserves in the building you are considering?

Neighborhood momentum matters, but the condominium itself still has to make sense.

Hotel Clark also illustrates the value of architecture

Los Angeles has historically been quick to demolish older buildings.

Adaptive reuse offers another option.

A historic building can retain the visual character of an older city while functioning for contemporary uses.

That can be especially valuable Downtown, where the mix of early twentieth-century architecture and newer towers creates a physical environment unlike most other Los Angeles neighborhoods.

The best adaptive-reuse projects do not simply preserve a facade. They give the building a reason to remain useful.

Frequently asked questions

Is Hotel Clark open? Yes. The property soft-opened in late September 2026 and is accepting reservations.

Where is Hotel Clark? 426 S. Hill Street in Downtown Los Angeles.

How many rooms does Hotel Clark have? 348 guestrooms.

What restaurant is at Hotel Clark? Votana is the property's Mediterranean-influenced restaurant and is expected to hold its larger opening celebration October 15.

Why is the opening notable? The historic property went through decades of redevelopment attempts and reportedly sat with much of its renovation completed for years before finally reopening.

Paul's take

I find Hotel Clark interesting because it shows how different real-estate timelines can be from what people expect.

From the street, a finished building can look like a project that simply needs someone to unlock the doors. The reality can be much more complicated.

Hotel Clark spent years representing unrealized potential. Now it is finally an operating property.

For Downtown, I think the most useful way to view the opening is not as evidence that the neighborhood has suddenly solved all of its problems. It is evidence that one more large, historic building has returned to productive use.

Those incremental changes are what I would continue watching.

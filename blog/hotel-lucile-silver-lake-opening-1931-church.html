Hotel Lucile Opens in a 1931 Silver Lake Church: What It Adds to the Neighborhood

By Paul Adams II · Los Angeles Real Estate Advisor · September 23, 2026

A 1931 church in Silver Lake has reopened with an entirely different use.

Hotel Lucile began welcoming guests September 14, transforming the former Bethany Presbyterian Church at 1629 Griffith Park Boulevard into a 25-room boutique hotel with a restaurant, rooftop deck, pool and art program.

The opening is worth paying attention to even if you never book a room. It is a particularly Los Angeles example of adaptive reuse, where an existing building is given a new economic life while retaining much of the architecture that made it distinctive.

What is Hotel Lucile?

Hotel Lucile is a 25-room boutique property operated by Casetta.

The building was originally designed as Bethany Presbyterian Church and dates to 1931. Hotel Lucile retained major architectural features including stained-glass windows, soaring arches and the former chapel.

Electric Bowery handled the architecture and interiors. Instead of attempting to make the building look like a conventional hotel, the conversion uses the church itself as the defining feature.

That is a much more interesting outcome than simply demolishing the structure and replacing it with a generic box.

Why does the building matter to Silver Lake?

The property has had several lives. It served as a church and later became an early home of East West Players, the Asian American theater company, before other uses followed. The conversion into a hotel took years and was not without neighborhood debate and financial complications.

That history is part of why adaptive reuse can be complicated. An old building may have character, but character does not automatically make redevelopment financially or technically easy.

Changing a building's use can involve accessibility, seismic, fire-life-safety, parking, neighborhood and entitlement issues. The result at Hotel Lucile demonstrates what can happen when the existing architecture becomes part of the business model rather than an obstacle to it.

What is Sous Sous?

The hotel's restaurant, Sous Sous, occupies the former chapel.

It comes from the team behind De La Nonna and opened with a menu combining Southern French influences, Southern California ingredients and elements of the American South. The room retains the church's stained glass and dramatic interior proportions.

For Silver Lake residents, this may ultimately matter more than the guestrooms. Hotels are primarily used by visitors. Restaurants become neighborhood amenities.

A successful restaurant can turn a lodging property into a place locals use rather than a building they simply walk past.

What else does the hotel include?

In addition to its 25 rooms and restaurant, the property includes a pool, rooftop deck, communal spaces and an art program featuring local and regional artists.

Its relatively small room count is significant. This is not a convention hotel dropped into a residential neighborhood.

It is closer to the scale of a large historic residence or institutional building that has been converted into hospitality. That gives the project a different relationship with the surrounding neighborhood.

Where is it?

Hotel Lucile is at 1629 Griffith Park Boulevard, close to Sunset Junction.

That places it within one of Silver Lake's most recognizable pedestrian-oriented commercial areas. Restaurants, cafes, the Silver Lake Farmers Market and neighborhood retail are nearby.

The location is part of the appeal. A visitor staying there can experience Silver Lake as a neighborhood rather than treating the hotel as an isolated destination.

Why do boutique hotels matter to residential neighborhoods?

Not every hotel improves a neighborhood, and not every resident wants more visitors. But small hotels can add a different type of economic activity than apartments or office buildings.

Visitors spend money at nearby restaurants and stores. A hotel restaurant can create another neighborhood gathering place. Historic rehabilitation can preserve architecture that otherwise may struggle to find an economically sustainable use.

There can also be tradeoffs. Hotel traffic, valet operations, noise, events and parking demand can affect nearby residents. Those impacts become particularly important when hospitality uses sit close to homes.

Does a hotel opening increase nearby home values?

I would not make that claim. One new hotel does not create a measurable automatic increase in the value of surrounding homes.

What it can do is contribute to the collection of amenities, architecture and businesses that shape how buyers perceive a neighborhood.

That is a softer relationship, but it is still relevant to real estate. People do not choose neighborhoods based only on bedroom count and price per square foot. They also choose them based on what it feels like to walk outside.

What does Hotel Lucile say about Silver Lake?

Silver Lake has long had a combination of residential streets and destination businesses. Hotel Lucile fits that pattern unusually well because the project itself is embedded in an older neighborhood building.

It also reinforces something that differentiates Silver Lake from newer master-planned districts. The neighborhood's identity often comes from reuse.

Older storefronts become restaurants. Bungalows become shops. Institutional buildings find new purposes. That can create the layered environment people associate with established Los Angeles neighborhoods.

Adaptive reuse is not just a Downtown Los Angeles story

When people hear adaptive reuse in Los Angeles, they often think about Downtown office buildings converted to apartments or hotels. But the principle works at smaller scales.

Churches, schools, warehouses, theaters and commercial buildings can all present opportunities for new uses when the original purpose no longer supports the property.

The difficulty is finding a use that works economically while respecting the building and its surroundings. Hotel Lucile is a particularly visible example because the former church remains unmistakable.

What should buyers notice when looking around Silver Lake?

I would look at the neighborhood at several levels. First, the individual house or condo. Second, the immediate block. Third, the commercial corridors you will actually use.

If a buyer values being able to walk to coffee, restaurants or a farmers market, proximity to Sunset Junction may matter. If the buyer values quiet above everything else, being directly adjacent to a busy commercial node may be less appealing.

Neither preference is right or wrong. Neighborhood amenities create value only when they match how the buyer wants to live.

How many rooms does Hotel Lucile have? It has 25 guestrooms.

What was the building originally? It was Bethany Presbyterian Church, built in 1931.

What restaurant is inside Hotel Lucile? Sous Sous, a restaurant from the De La Nonna team, occupies the former chapel.

Paul's take

I like this project because the interesting part is not simply that Silver Lake got another hotel. It is what happened to the building.

Los Angeles loses a lot when every obsolete property is treated as a blank site. Sometimes demolition makes sense. Sometimes a building has enough architectural or cultural value that finding a new use produces something much more distinctive.

Hotel Lucile preserved a piece of Silver Lake's physical history while introducing a use that can contribute to the neighborhood today. That is the kind of development that makes a neighborhood more interesting to follow.

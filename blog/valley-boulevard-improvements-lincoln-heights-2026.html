Valley Boulevard Is Getting a $9.4 Million Makeover: What It Could Mean for Lincoln Heights

By Paul Adams II · Los Angeles Real Estate Advisor · October 2, 2026

A major stretch of Valley Boulevard is slated for a substantial public-space upgrade.

The California Transportation Commission has awarded $9.4 million toward improvements along approximately 4.5 miles of Valley Boulevard between Union Station and the I-710 terminus.

Plans include protected bike lanes, peak-hour bus lanes, new sidewalks, hundreds of pedestrian and street lights, approximately 1,800 trees, new landscaping and a two-acre extension of Lincoln Park connecting with Parque de Mexico.

For Lincoln Heights property owners and buyers, the interesting part is not simply the funding announcement. It is what a corridor can become when transportation, pedestrian infrastructure and public space are upgraded together.

What exactly is planned for Valley Boulevard?

The project is unusually broad.

Current plans call for protected bike lanes with landscaped medians, peak-hour bus-only lanes, approximately nine miles of new sidewalks, roughly 800 pedestrian and street lights, approximately 1,800 trees, three acres of landscaping, and a two-acre extension of Lincoln Park that would connect with Parque de Mexico.

That is much more than repaving a street. It is a redesign of how pedestrians, cyclists, transit riders and drivers use the corridor.

Where will the improvements happen?

The project covers roughly 4.5 miles between Union Station and the I-710 freeway terminus.

That places Lincoln Heights within a corridor connecting Northeast Los Angeles with Downtown and areas farther east.

Valley Boulevard currently functions heavily as a transportation route. The planned improvements attempt to make portions of it work better as a neighborhood street as well.

That distinction matters for nearby properties. A street designed primarily to move vehicles tends to interact with adjacent homes and businesses differently from one designed to accommodate walking, cycling, transit and landscaping.

Where is the money coming from?

The California Transportation Commission announced more than $2.7 billion in transportation funding statewide.

District 7, covering Los Angeles and Ventura Counties, is receiving roughly $40 million across several projects.

Valley Boulevard received $9.4 million in the latest allocation. The corridor had previously received another $9 million through other state programs and is also drawing from local funds originally associated with the unbuilt 710 freeway extension.

That funding history matters because large infrastructure projects often depend on multiple funding sources rather than one check.

Why does Lincoln Park matter?

One of the most interesting components is the planned two-acre expansion of Lincoln Park. The extension would connect with Parque de Mexico.

Public green space is especially valuable in neighborhoods where private yards may be small or nonexistent. An expanded park can provide recreational space, shade and a stronger pedestrian connection between nearby destinations.

Again, I would not translate that automatically into a specific increase in property values. But proximity to usable parks is something many buyers consider when choosing between neighborhoods.

What could 1,800 new trees change?

Potentially quite a bit at street level.

Trees can provide shade, improve the pedestrian environment and visually soften a heavily trafficked corridor. The effect is not immediate. Young trees take years to mature, and long-term maintenance determines whether a landscaping program actually succeeds.

But 1,800 trees across a 4.5-mile corridor represents a meaningful landscape intervention rather than a few decorative plantings. For people walking to businesses, transit stops or parks, shade can materially affect how comfortable a street feels.

What about the bus lanes?

Peak-hour bus-only lanes could improve transit reliability along the corridor. That matters because transportation access is one of the variables that can affect how residents perceive a location.

A buyer may care about freeway access. Another may care about rail. Another may rely on buses. Infrastructure that improves multiple transportation options can broaden who finds a neighborhood practical.

But implementation matters. A bus lane that functions well is different from one that is poorly enforced or creates confusing traffic patterns. The eventual operating details will be worth watching.

Could the bike lanes change the corridor?

Protected bike lanes are different from painted bike lanes. Physical separation can make cycling feel more accessible to people who would not otherwise be comfortable riding next to moving traffic.

If the network connects to useful destinations rather than ending abruptly, it can become a meaningful transportation option. The larger question is connectivity.

Similar bus, bike, pedestrian and landscaping improvements are planned for nearby Huntington Drive and Eastern Avenue using funding once associated with the 710 extension. That begins to look more like a network than a single improved street.

What could this mean for local businesses?

A more pedestrian-friendly corridor can change how people interact with storefronts.

Someone driving at speed is less likely to notice a small business than someone walking, cycling or waiting for transit. Street trees, lighting and sidewalks can make it easier to move between businesses rather than driving from one destination to another.

But construction can also be disruptive. Lane closures, sidewalk work and access changes can temporarily hurt businesses before improvements are completed. That tradeoff is important. The long-term vision may be attractive while the construction period is difficult.

What should buyers near Valley Boulevard investigate?

First, determine how close the property actually is to the improvement area. Second, understand whether that proximity is likely to be an amenity or inconvenience for your lifestyle.

A buyer who values transit, cycling and walkability may prefer to be closer. Someone who prioritizes quiet residential streets may prefer being several blocks removed.

Third, investigate nearby land use. Infrastructure investment can make redevelopment more attractive, but zoning and parcel configuration still determine what can actually be built.

Does infrastructure investment increase property values?

Not automatically. This is where real-estate discussions can become too simplistic.

Public investment may make an area more attractive. But property values also depend on housing supply, interest rates, local schools, property condition, zoning, crime, employment, broader market conditions and many other factors.

The better way to think about infrastructure is as one component of neighborhood desirability. A safer sidewalk or improved park may matter to a buyer even if no one can isolate its exact dollar value.

Why should investors pay attention?

Infrastructure can change the long-term utility of a location.

For an investor evaluating a small apartment property or mixed-use building, I would want to know whether the surrounding public realm is improving or deteriorating. I would also want to understand construction timing.

Buying directly on a corridor before years of construction may create near-term operational headaches. Buying near a completed improvement may have different economics. The investment thesis should account for both.

Lincoln Heights is worth watching beyond this project

Lincoln Heights has an unusual location. It sits immediately northeast of Downtown, near major freeways, rail and the Los Angeles River. Yet it retains a different scale and character from Downtown itself.

Transportation and streetscape investment can strengthen those connections without necessarily turning Lincoln Heights into an extension of Downtown.

That tension is part of what makes established Los Angeles neighborhoods interesting. Improved connectivity does not erase local identity unless development and planning decisions make it do so.

What happens next?

The funding announcement is a major step, but it is not the same as a completed project.

Design, contracting, construction phasing and implementation will determine when residents actually see the improvements. That means this is a story worth revisiting.

The most useful future update will not be another funding announcement. It will be when construction begins, when major segments open, or when the city provides a clear implementation timeline.

Frequently asked questions

How much funding did Valley Boulevard receive? The California Transportation Commission awarded $9.4 million in the latest funding round.

How long is the project corridor? Approximately 4.5 miles between Union Station and the I-710 terminus.

What improvements are planned? Protected bike lanes, peak-hour bus lanes, new sidewalks, lighting, approximately 1,800 trees, landscaping and a two-acre expansion of Lincoln Park.

Is Lincoln Park being expanded? Plans call for a two-acre extension connecting Lincoln Park with Parque de Mexico.

When will construction begin? The current funding announcement does not establish a complete construction schedule. That should be monitored as implementation advances.

Paul's take

This is the type of public investment I think property owners should watch even when they are not planning to sell.

Neighborhood change is not only about new apartment buildings. Sidewalks matter. Trees matter. Transit matters. Parks matter. Lighting matters.

Individually, none determines the value of a property. Together, they shape how people experience a neighborhood.

The Valley Boulevard project is particularly interesting because it combines several of those elements across a long corridor rather than improving one intersection at a time.
